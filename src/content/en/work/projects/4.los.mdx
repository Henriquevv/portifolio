## 1. The Business Problem
The unpredictability of Length of Stay (LOS) is one of the biggest bottlenecks in hospital operations. When we don't know when the patient will be discharged, we are unable to plan bed rotation, which leads to overcrowding in the Emergency Room and cancellation of elective surgeries.

This project was developed as a case study in Data Science, simulating real scenarios to demonstrate how the use of data can reduce these uncertainties.

## 2. Solution Proposal
The objective is to move away from the simple average (which hides serious cases) and use Artificial Intelligence to support clinical decision-making. To achieve this, I structured the analysis on three fronts:

1.  Flow Visibility (Survival Analysis): Map the probability of patient discharge day after day, allowing you to predict the release of beds.
2.  Risk Screening (Classification): Identify, upon admission, which patients are at high risk of long stay (> 8 days) so that the NIR/Discharge Management can act preventively.
3.  Diagnosis of Causes (Inference): Understanding which factors (clinical or operational) are "holding back" the patient in the hospital.

### 4. Construction of the Analytical Base and Variable Engineering

Simulated data for this study, inspired by real extractions from management systems (ERP), often contain noise that distorts indicators. In this step, I processed the simulated raw base to ensure that the model learns from the represented operational reality, and not from registration inconsistencies.

Strategic Transformations:

* Temporal Sanitation: Strict standardization of entry and exit dates. This prevents typing errors (e.g. changing days for months) from creating "artificial outliers" that would harm the average length of stay.
* Actual Occupancy Calculation (LOS): Definition of length of stay ($\Delta t$). Adjusted "same day discharge" cases to ensure that every patient accounts for resource consumption, eliminating null values.
* Management of Active Patients (Censorship): A difference from Survival Analysis. I created the event variable ($E$) to teach the model to distinguish:
    * $E=1$: Closed cycle (Patient was discharged/death).
    * $E=0$: Censorship (Patient *is still in bed*). This prevents the model from thinking that an ongoing long hospitalization is an error or missing data.
* Statistical Stabilization (Log-Transform): As the length of stay does not follow a normal curve (many quick patients, few very long ones), I applied a logarithmic transformation (`np.log1p`). This balances the weight of edge cases, allowing the model to learn majority patterns without ignoring complex cases.

### 5. Dwelling Behavior (EDA)

Before predicting the future, it is crucial to understand the present. In this step, I analyzed the actual Dwell Time (LOS) distribution to establish our baseline for comparison.

Structural Insights:

* The "Mean Trap" (Asymmetric Distribution): I confirmed that the hospital data has a "right-skewed" shape. Few patients with very long stays push the average upwards, making it not representative of the typical patient.
    * *Technical Action:* I applied logarithmic transformations to normalize the data. This prevents the model from being biased by *outliers* and mispredicting the vast majority of services.

* The Comparison Ruler (Kaplan-Meier Global): I constructed the Global Survival Curve to answer the question: *"What is the statistical probability of any patient remaining hospitalized after day X?"*.
    * *Purpose:* This curve acts as our "zero". Any group of patients (e.g. Elderly, Surgical) that deviates greatly from this curve will be identified as a point of attention or efficiency.

### Staying Behavior Diagnosis (EDA)

The exploratory analysis served to validate the quality of the data and, mainly, to define the modeling strategy. The data revealed that treating hospitalization as a simple normal distribution would lead to prediction errors.

Key Findings:

#### The "Average Trap"
I identified a critical discrepancy between the core metrics:
* Average: 5.35 days.
* Median: 2.00 days.

Business Insight: The “typical” patient only stays 2 days. The average is inflated (more than double the median) due to a few cases of very long stays. Using the simple average for capacity planning would generate serious distortions, allocating the wrong resources to the majority of patients.

#### Identification of the Long Tail (Asymmetry)
The histogram confirmed that the operation deals with two very distinct occupancy profiles:
* Rapid Turnover (0-5 days): The vast majority of cases.
* Long Tail (Extreme): Highly complex cases that, although less frequent, disproportionately consume available bed days.

#### Technical Correction (Logarithmic Transformation)
To deal with this asymmetry, I applied the `np.log1p` function to the target variable.
* Objective: Compress the scale of extreme values ​​(very high days) to bring the distribution closer to a normal curve. This prevents the regression algorithm from getting "confused" by trying to adjust the error for just the edge cases, improving overall accuracy.

Strategic Decision:
Given the confirmed high variability, I concluded that a pure regression model (predicting the exact day) will have natural limitations. Therefore, the most robust strategy for the hospital is to act on two fronts: estimating the duration, but mainly Classifying the Risk, segregating patients from "Quick Turn" vs. "Long Stay" (> 8 days).

### 6. Data Refinement and Intelligence Creation

For the model to see complex patterns, it is not enough to deliver the system's raw data. Performed an Attribute Engineering process to translate administrative records into tangible risk factors for the algorithm.

Enrichment Strategies:

1.  Seasonal Context (`IS_INVERNO`):
    I created a specific indicator for the period from June to September. This teaches the model that, in these months, the complexity of cases naturally increases (due to respiratory diseases), adjusting the stay prediction upwards regardless of the base diagnosis.

2.  Vital Signs Sanitation:
    Implemented security filters for physiological data. Impossible values ​​(e.g., zero blood pressure or saturation) were treated as a measurement/typing error and statistically corrected. This prevents the model from confusing a registration error in triage with a dying or highly serious patient.

3.  Clinical Data Translation:
    Mathematical algorithms do not interpret text. I converted qualitative variables (such as Specialty and Agreement) into binary numerical representations (*One-Hot Encoding*), allowing the model to weight the exact weight of each specialty in the occupation time.

4.  Validation Protocol (Stratified Split):
    I separated 20% of the data for testing. The division was made ensuring that the proportion of discharged patients vs. hospitalized patients was identical in both groups, faithfully simulating the mix of patients that the hospital receives on a daily basis.

### 7. Estimation of Bed Days: Regression Modeling

At this stage, the objective was to answer the most critical operational question: *"How many days, exactly, should this patient occupy the bed?"*. I treated the problem as a regression task to estimate continuous LOS.

Strategic Modeling Decisions:

* The Engine (Random Forest): I chose an *ensemble* model (random forest) for its ability to understand human complexity.
    * *Why:* Health is not linear. The combination of factors (e.g. *Advanced Age* + *Heart Failure*) generates an exponential risk, not just a simple sum. Linear models would fail to capture this nuance, but decision trees excel at this.

* The Success Metric (MAE - Average Error): To evaluate the model, I prioritized the Average Absolute Error.
    * *Justification:* As we saw in the exploratory analysis, we have extreme cases (hospitalizations lasting months). Traditional metrics (such as RMSE) would give excessive weight to these rare cases, skewing accuracy for the "typical" 3-day patient. MAE maintains focus on day-to-day performance.

Expectation Management:
Biological recovery has unpredictable (stochastic) variables. The objective of this model is not to "guess the future" with minute precision, but to minimize the average error to provide reliable planning guidance for the bed management team.

### Discussion of Results: Limits of Accurate Prediction

At this stage, I evaluated the ability of the regression model (*Random Forest*) to answer the question: *"On what exact day will this patient be discharged?"*. The results show a clear dichotomy between routine patients and complex cases.

Performance Diagnosis:

#### The “Blind Spot” of Admission (R² ≈ 18%)
The model can explain 18.4% of the variability in length of stay.
* Business Interpretation: This is not a defect in the model, but a characteristic of the sector. Admission data (who the patient is) defines the beginning of the journey, but what really determines discharge are the intra-hospital events (response to medications, availability of tests, complications) that have not yet occurred.

#### Efficiency in the Typical Patient (MAE vs. RMSE)
Error metrics tell two different stories:
* For day to day (MAE = 3.67 days): The mean absolute error is low. For the vast majority of patients (low/medium complexity cases), the model is a reliable tool for flow planning.
* For extreme cases (RMSE = 9.45 days): The squared error jumps due to *outliers*. The model loses accuracy when trying to quantify very long hospitalizations (e.g. predicting whether you will stay for 40 or 60 days).

#### Visual Analysis: Conservatism in Serious Cases
The scatterplot reveals that the algorithm is conservative:
* In cases of long stays, it tends to underestimate the real time (predicts fewer days than actually occurs).
* Diagnosis: The model can detect *that* the patient will take time, but has difficulty determining *how much*.

Strategic Pivot:
Insisting on predicting the exact discharge day for critically ill patients risks frustration for the care team. The smartest decision is to change the question. Instead of *"What day does he leave?"*, we will focus on *"Is this patient High Risk (> 8 days)?"*.

This takes us to the next step: Risk Classification, where we transform the regression uncertainty into a binary alert system for the NIR (Internal Regulation Core).

### 8. Identifying Bottlenecks: Comparison of Clinical Profiles

We know that “there is no such thing as the average patient.” An elective simple surgery patient does not behave like an acute emergency room patient. In this step, I used the Kaplan-Meier estimator to segment the survival curve and prove where the occupancy bottlenecks are.

Analysis Strategy:

* Flow Visualization (Stratified Curves):
    I plotted separate discharge curves for different groups (e.g. *Elective vs. Urgent*).
    * *What we are looking for:* If the curves visually move apart, it means that one group rotates the bed much faster than the other, requiring different management goals.

* Mathematical Test (Log-Rank Test):
    Não basta o gráfico parecer diferente; é preciso ter certeza estatística.
    * *Application:* I used the Log-Rank test to calculate whether the difference in length of stay between the groups is real or the result of chance.
    * *Decision:* If the p-value is < 0.05, we scientifically confirm that that factor (e.g.: Diagnosis) is a critical determinant of the delay in discharge.



### Discussion of Results: Where are the Bottlenecks?

Segmentation via Kaplan-Meier revealed that the hospital operates, in practice, with "two hospitals" within one, each with radically opposite occupancy dynamics.

Operational Diagnosis:

#### The Dichotomy of Care (Emergency vs. Elective)
Survival curves expose two distinct realities:
* Elective Flow (High Efficiency): Curve with an abrupt drop in the first 48 hours.
    * *Insight:* Scheduled procedures rotate the bed quickly and predictably. They are the "locomotive" of revenue and efficiency.
* Urgency Flow (Bottleneck): Smooth curve with long tail.
    * *Insight:* Unscheduled patients have a longer median length of stay (~4 days) and high variability. They are the occupation's primary offenders, requiring individualized case management.

#### The Burden of Chronic Diseases (ICD Analysis)
I identified that the entry diagnosis dictates the pace of discharge:
* Bed Retainers: Chapters 'I' (Circulatory) and 'J' (Respiratory) presented the worst discharge curves. Patients with cardiac or pulmonary decompensation are structurally slower to recover, requiring specific dehospitalization protocols.
* Rapid Turnover: In contrast, chapter 'K' (Digestive) showed rapid highs ($t < 2$ days), validating the surgical/resolutive profile.

#### Scientific Validation
The *Log-Rank* test confirmed ($p < 0.0001$) that these differences are not random.
* Business Conclusion: The origin of the patient (Emergency) and the underlying disease are critical determinants. A predictive model that ignores these variables will be blind to the reality of the hospital. We therefore validate that they must be the main *features* (predictor variables) in the Machine Learning stage.

### 9. Triage Solution: Identification of Critical Patients

Given the difficulty in predicting the exact day of discharge for complex cases (the regression "blind spot"), I adopted a complementary approach: Risk Discretization.

If we can't guarantee *when* the patient leaves, we need to make sure the hospital knows *who* are the patients who are going to stay a long time.

Definition of the New Strategy:

* The Business Question (Binary Target): The model stops trying to hit a number and starts calculating the probability of a critical scenario: *"Does this patient have a chance of exceeding 8 days of hospitalization?"*.
    * *Why 8 days?* This value represents the upper quartile of our distribution. Patients who exceed this mark are the biggest offenders of bed turnover.

* The Engine (XGBoost Classifier): I used the XGBoost algorithm, an industry standard for classifying unbalanced data (where "success" cases are the minority), to separate the wheat from the chaff.

Success Criteria: The “Error Safe” Policy
In hospital management, not every error carries the same weight.
* The Acceptable Error (False Positive): The model says that the patient is serious, the team audits it, but he is quickly discharged. *Cost: Administrative time.*
* The Unacceptable Error (False Negative): The model says that the patient is simple, no one looks, and he spends 20 days occupying the bed without planning. *Cost: Bed blocking and no assistance.*

Decision: I optimized the model focusing on Recall (Sensitivity). The goal is to create a "safety net" that captures as many seriously ill patients as possible, even if this generates some false alerts to check.

By default, algorithms expect 50% certainty to issue an alert. For the hospital environment, I considered this risky.
* Business Decision: Reduced the alert trigger to 35% probability.
* Impact: With this adjustment, we increased the model's sensitivity (Recall) from 76% to 84%. In practice, this creates a "fine mesh" that captures the vast majority of complex cases, preventing critically ill patients from going unmanaged.

#### The Cost of Security (Trade-off)

* The Reality: Out of every 3 "High Risk" alerts, 1 will actually be a critical long stay.
* Justification: The operational cost of the team checking 2 additional patients (false positives) is negligible compared to the financial and healthcare loss of letting a critical patient occupy a bed for weeks without planning.

Conclusion:
The model is calibrated to act as a High Sensitivity Radar. It prioritizes safety, ensuring that the bed management team receives early alerts about the patients who will require the most resources.

### 11. Reliability Audit: Is the Model “Honest”?

Em saúde, não basta o modelo acertar a classificação (Alta vs. Baixa); a confiança dele precisa ser realista. Se o algoritmo diz que um paciente tem 80% de risco, esperamos que, estatisticamente, 80% dos pacientes com essa nota realmente tenham longa permanência.

To verify this, I used the Calibration Curve (Reliability Diagram), which works as a "lie detector" for mathematical probability.

How to read this diagnosis:

* The Line of Truth (Dotted Diagonal): Represents perfect calibration. If the model's curve follows this line, it "knows what it says."
* "Alarmism" Bias (Below the line): If the model curve is below the diagonal, it is overestimating the risk (it says it is 90%, but in reality it only happens in 60% of cases).
* "Negligence" Bias (Above the line): If it is above, he is underestimating the danger (he says it's easy, but the patient complicates it).

### 13. Opening the Black Box: The “Why” of the Decision

One of the biggest risks in Artificial Intelligence is the lack of transparency. To ensure the trust of the clinical team, I used the SHAP technique to audit the internal \"reasoning\" of the model.

This analysis reveals exactly how much and how each variable (Age, Vital Signs, Origin) influenced the final prediction, allowing you to validate whether the algorithm is following the correct medical logic.

How to read the "Influence Map" (Beeswarm Plot):

* The Golden Rule:
    * Points on the RIGHT (Positive) = Factors that increase the length of stay (Aggravating).
    * Points on the LEFT (Negatives) = Factors that accelerate the rise (Protectors).

* Color Indicates Intensity:
    * Red (High): High value of the variable (ex: Old age).
    * Blue (Low): Low value of the variable (ex: Young patient).

Example of Expected Reading: If we see *red* dots (High Age) accumulated on the *right* side (Increases LOS), the model correctly learned that aging is a risk factor.

### 14. Cause Mapping: What really holds the patient back?

Para atacar o problema da longa permanência, não basta saber *quem* vai ficar; precisamos entender o *porquê*.

I used Cox Regression (Multivariate) to isolate the impact of each variable. Unlike the previous analysis, this model "discounts" cross effects (e.g. it separates the weight of Age from the weight of Disease), revealing the true offenders of bed turnover.

How to interpret the Results (Hazard Ratio - HR):

The model calculates a high speed index. The neutral point is 1.0.
* Offenders (HR < 1.0): Factors that "hold" the patient in the hospital. The lower the number, the worse the impact.
    * *Example:* If "Emergency" has HR 0.5, it means that these patients have half the discharge speed of elective patients.
* Accelerators (HR > 1.0): Factors associated with rapid increases.
    * *Example:* If an ICD has HR 2.0, these patients turn the bed twice as fast as the average.

Objective: Identify which clinical or administrative factors have the greatest retention power in order to focus efficiency protocols on them.


```python

from lifelines import CoxPHFitter

cols_cox_base = ['IDADE', 'SEXO', 'CID_CAPITULO', 'CARATER_ATENDIMENTO']
cols_comorb = [c for c in cols_flags if c in df_mestre.columns]

df_cox = df_mestre[['LOS', 'Evento_ocorreu'] + cols_cox_base + cols_comorb].copy()


df_cox['IDADE'] = df_cox['IDADE'].fillna(df_cox['IDADE'].median())
df_cox = df_cox.dropna()

df_cox_dummy = pd.get_dummies(df_cox, drop_first=True)

df_cox_dummy.columns = [c.replace(' ', '_').replace('/', '_').replace('-', '_') for c in df_cox_dummy.columns]

print(f"Dataset Cox pronto. Dimensões: {df_cox_dummy.shape}")

cph = CoxPHFitter(penalizer=0.1)

try:
    cph.fit(df_cox_dummy, duration_col='LOS', event_col='Evento_ocorreu')

    plt.figure(figsize=(10, 8))
    cph.plot()
    plt.title("Hazard Ratios (Risco Relativo de Alta)")
    plt.axvline(x=0, color='red', linestyle='--')
    plt.xlabel("Impacto na Probabilidade de Alta (log-scale)")
    plt.show()

    print("\nResumo Estatístico (P-values e HR)")
    summary = cph.summary
    display(summary[summary['p'] < 0.05].sort_values('coef'))

except Exception as e:
    print(f"Não foi possível ajustar o Cox (provavelmente multicolinearidade): {e}")
```

### Discussion of Results: What Impacts Length of Hospitalization?

Using multivariate analysis (Cox Regression), we were able to isolate the real impact of each variable on the patient's probability of discharge. Below, we translate the statistical coefficients (*Hazard Ratios*) into clear operational impacts.

#### The Big Bottleneck: Emergency Admissions (HR ≈ 0.21)
The `CARATER_ATENDIMENTO_URGENCIA` variable has proven to be the biggest offender of bed turnover.
* What the data says: A patient admitted through emergency has an 80% lower probability of daily discharge than an elective patient with similar characteristics.
* Impact on the Business: This statistically confirms that the unpredictability of the entry point (Emergency) is the main cause of bed retention. Processes designed for elective patients don't work here.

#### Clinical Complexity as a Speed ​​Reducer
The model quantified exactly how much chronic conditions “brake” discharge:
* Oncology (HR ≈ 0.54): Patients with neoplasia have their discharge rate reduced by 46%. It is the most important clinical factor, requiring dedicated case management.
* Diabetes (HR ≈ 0.56): The presence of diabetes reduces the chance of discharge by 44%, indicating that the clinical stabilization of this comorbidity consumes precious days of hospitalization.

#### The Weight of Age in Occupation
* Age (HR ≈ 0.992): Aging acts as a progressive risk factor. For every 10 additional years of life, the agility at discharge drops by around 8%. Although it may seem small in isolation, in elderly patients this effect accumulates, making them long-term consumers by nature.

#### Rapid Turnaround Opportunities (HR > 1.0)
We identify profiles that accelerate the flow and can be optimized:
* External Causes (Chapter V): Presented the greatest potential for increase (HR ≈ 2.92). Patients in this group (e.g. minor accidents) turn almost 3x faster than average.
* Injuries and Traumas (Chapter T): With an HR of 1.33, they also indicate a rapid resolution profile.

Conclusion and Suggested Strategy:
The analysis proves that Length of Stay (LOS) is strongly penalized by Origin (Urgency) and Disease Burden.
From a management point of view, the data suggests that mixing these profiles in the same flow generates inefficiency.

## 16. Length of Stay (LOS) Forecast: Data-Driven Strategy

## The Business Challenge
Predictability in bed occupancy is one of the biggest bottlenecks in hospital management. The uncertainty about when a patient will be discharged impacts everything from the Emergency Room flow to the planning of elective surgeries.

This project aimed to answer the question: Is it possible to anticipate which patients will have a long (high cost) hospitalization using only the data available at admission?

## Strategy and Methodology
To ensure that the data reflected operational reality, I carried out a rigorous treatment of the database, correcting temporal inconsistencies (errors in recording dates) that could bias the analysis.

I adopted a hybrid approach to solve the problem:
Diagnosis: Use of Survival Analysis to understand discharge behavior over time.
Modeling: Testing two scenarios — trying to predict the exact day of discharge (Regression) versus identifying the risk of long stay (Classification).

## Main Discoveries (Insights)

Statistical analysis revealed the hospital's operational bottlenecks:
* The Urgency Factor: The patient’s origin is decisive. Patients admitted by emergency have an 80% lower probability of daily discharge than elective patients. This statistically validates the Emergency Department as the critical point for bed retention.
* Clinical Offenders: We identified that Neoplasms and Diabetes reduce the speed of discharge by around 45%, regardless of the patient's age.
* Rotation Opportunity: Diagnoses of External Causes (e.g. minor trauma) rotate the bed almost 3 times faster than average.

## Model Results

### Strategic Decision: Classification vs. Regression
When comparing the approaches, the Risk Classification proved to be superior for managerial decision making.

* The Regression Limit (Random Forest): The model was able to predict the length of stay with an average error of 3.6 days. Although useful for simple cases, it loses precision in extreme cases (precisely those that impact the cost the most).
* The Strength of Classification (XGBoost): We chose to focus on identifying *who* will stay longer than 8 days. The model achieved excellent discrimination capacity (AUC-ROC of 0.87).

### Operational Impact
I adjusted the model to prioritize care safety (Sensitivity):
* The algorithm was able to detect 84% of long-stay patients upon entry.
* This allows the bed management team to act preventively in the majority of critical cases, without waiting for the patient to "explode" the expected time.

### Transparency (White Box)
To ensure the trust of the medical team, I used SHAP Values ​​to explain the model's decisions. The tool confirmed that the algorithm follows clinical logic: it learned that Advanced Age, Instability in Vital Signs and Emergency Admission are the biggest risk vectors.

## Conclusion
The study proved that basic admission data has high predictive power. The implementation of this Risk Screening model makes it possible to segregate flows: create fast tracks (*Fast Tracks*) for simple cases and allocate early dehospitalization resources for complex cases identified by the algorithm.

### 17. Limitations and Opportunities for Evolution

Although the model is functional for initial screening, it is important to highlight points of attention for its implementation and evolution:

* Specific Context: The algorithm learned the operational and clinical patterns of a single hospital. To scale the solution to other units, re-training would be necessary to capture local specificities (processes and demographic profile).
* Data Depth: The model only uses data available at intake. The inclusion of intra-hospital variables (laboratory tests, nursing progress or scores) would certainly increase precision.
* Static Prediction ("Door Photo"): The risk is calculated only at the entrance. As the clinical picture is dynamic, a future evolution of the project would be to recalculate the score daily, adjusting the prediction as the patient improves or worsens.
* Validation in Production: We use cross-validation to ensure technical robustness. The ideal next step would be a pilot test (*Out-of-Time* validation) to monitor the stability of the model against new and seasonal data before the full *rollout*.
